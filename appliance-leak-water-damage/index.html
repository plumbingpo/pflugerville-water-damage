Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in Pflugerville

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. Washer supply hoses can burst and keep spraying at full pressure until the valve is closed. With a dishwasher the leak is typically slow, coming from the door seal or the drain hose where the cabinets hide it. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. No two of these call for the same cleanup.

Our Pflugerville crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. With a laundry leak we look at the rooms next to it and the wall behind the washer, plus the ceiling below when the laundry sits on an upper floor. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Right Now

What to Do Right Now

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceIf the outlet and the floor next to it are both dry, end the cycle and take the plug out of the wall. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineFor a washing machine, close both the cold and hot valves on the wall at its back. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. A refrigerator line has a small valve below or behind the unit.
  3. Mop Up What You CanUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Photograph the Failed PartKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.
Insurance

Does Insurance Cover It?

Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental events, and a burst hose or a machine overflowing with no warning is that kind of event. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. Some insurers also ask about the age and upkeep of the hose. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • The drain hose of the washer pulling free of the standpipe, or the standpipe backing up from a clog.
  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
  • Washer supply hoses made of rubber that get weak over the years and burst from the pressure.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
What Is at Risk

Where the Damage Spreads

Floors and What Is Under Them

A leak runs under laminate, hardwood or vinyl plank flooring with little resistance. Boards cup or swell, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the surface looks dry.

Cabinet Damage

From below, a leaking dishwasher wets the toe kicks and the side panels of the cabinets. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Water standing on a laundry or kitchen floor wicks up into baseboards and the bottom of the drywall. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Adjoining Rooms

A washer hose can release water for hours. Hallways, bedrooms and closets get wet as it travels under the walls, and carpet and pad are soaked a good distance from the laundry.
